In Plato's [*Phaedrus*](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedrus.html),
the god Theuth invents writing and brings it before King Thamus, expecting
praise. Instead, Thamus warns that this new invention will not make people 
wiser, only more convinced of their own wisdom. Those who rely on it, 
he says, will carry the appearance of knowledge without ever doing the work 
of possessing it.

> They will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing; they will 
> be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the reality.
>
> — Plato, *Phaedrus*

Most importantly here, Plato is not saying that reading is useless. The
myth itself describes writing as useful as a *reminder* for someone who
already knows something. The skepticism is aimed towards treating this
written record as a substitute for _understanding_.

I think one of the more dangerous effects that LLMs will have on society
probably _isn't_ the uprising of killer bots or the replacement of jobs
but rather that *the pursuit of knowledge will effectively be killed*. 

And I think what's more disappointing is that there are many who are
closer to my age, fresh out of college or just starting, that have a deep
yearning to master a particular concept, field of work or study, that
unfortunately give up incredibly early because they see the objectively
miraculous things that LLMs can do, and think "it's probably not conducive
for me to learn this thing anymore."

A huge factor in this public fervor is of course, the depraved and 
degenerate echo chamber that is X (and other social media forums and 
channels). Beyond that, others just prefer that they can keep a roof 
over their heads and live a life worth enjoying and passing that life on 
to their children. That's always been the case with Computer Science in
particular; many graduated from their degree and did close to nothing
outside of work because their jobs were just that-- a place to show up
to 5 days out of the week to earn a paycheck in return.

But, it would be a disservice to chalk this up to laziness or 
instrumentalism. There is something deeper, that I think many lament,
about the fact that since the rise of these tools, most notably
for software engineers and others working in this space, you often end 
your week with a net-zero gain in knowledge. Despite getting your work
done, or even having generated a considerable amount of income, you
can't really take inventory of the new things you've learned.

For the little you do learn, it makes negligible difference on your
career prospects due to the competitive nature of the market right now.
And here, it becomes hard for me to understand why someone might refuse 
to put time and effort into understanding their work.

Perhaps the useful distinction to land on is that of Plato, however many
centuries ago. Writing was not worthless because it externalized memory and
likewise, LLMs are not dangerous because they externalize parts of reasoning.
But, when the output of the tool becomes indistinguishable, in our own minds,
from something we understand, that is when we should begin to ruminate
on this issue.

I know that an LLM can (and probably will) do my job better than me. We can
even operate under the assumption that this is inevitable. But I don't want
to merely "do my job". It does not make sense that because a _machine can_, 
that _we shouldn't_.
